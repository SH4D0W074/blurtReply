# Pairwise demonstration

## A 60-second pitch

“Five students choose the right answer. Have they understood the same thing?

Pairwise asks one more question: why? Watch these two explanations. Both students said no, but one distinguishes sampling uncertainty from recruitment bias. The other says bigger samples never reduce random error.

Pairwise’s AI suggests what each explanation expresses, quotes the evidence and asks for clarification when it is unsure. The lecturer checks those suggestions before revealing the reasons and forming discussion groups.

Students compare their thinking with someone in the room. Then each answers a different question independently. We show what they actually wrote, without claiming one round proves learning.

This is our labelled demonstration. The same flow also accepts real student responses. Pairwise makes the reasoning behind an answer visible, and gives students a reason to talk.”

## Rehearsal

1. Open Pairwise. The home preview flips two identical choices into contrasting explanations.
2. Leave the statistics lesson selected; choose **Try the demo**. Six labelled examples appear.
3. Open **Present** in a second window. Project this window; keep the lecturer workspace on your own screen.
4. Choose **Review the reasoning**. In the lecturer view, inspect the suggested groups and their quotations. On the Responses tab, opening an answer shows the authored interpretation.
5. Choose **Reveal the reasons**. The presentation cards turn over. Point to Birch and Cedar, who both chose B but gave incompatible claims about random uncertainty.
6. Choose **Start the discussion**. The same answer objects are arranged into groups with a concrete discussion prompt. Use the presentation paging controls if needed.
7. Choose **Open the fresh question**. State that the six follow-up examples are authored demo data. In a real session, this step waits for the students’ own submissions.
8. Choose **See the class reflection**. Show the actual counts and two written explanations. Different questions are not a validated learning-gain measure.
9. Use **Restart the demo** if another judge wants to try it. This clears the room’s responses; confirm only when ready.

## A real participant demonstration

- Create a session with **Start a session**. Keep the original browser as lecturer.
- Open the student link in another browser or incognito context on the same computer. Join, choose an answer, and explain it in a sentence. A second independent context gives a second student.
- If live AI is enabled, the review uses the configured OpenAI model. Otherwise it explicitly falls back to unclassified comparison groups. Do not present that fallback as AI inference.
- The server is local to this computer by default. A phone cannot join a loopback URL. The UI therefore does not show a misleading phone QR code in local mode.

## Questions judges may ask

**What does the AI do?** It classifies the claim explicitly expressed in a short explanation against a teacher-authored rubric. It returns an allowed category and an exact quotation. Unclear input gets a clarification probe. Grouping is deterministic and uses only authored contrasting category pairs.

**Why not use a chatbot?** The product coordinates a whole-room sequence: independent collection, private lecturer review, a deliberate public reveal, peer discussion and fresh responses. The shared object is the class’s reasoning, not a chat history.

**What if it is wrong or the network fails?** Interpretations remain suggestions. The lecturer sees the quoted evidence, can use neutral comparison groups, and controls publication. Responses are saved locally before any AI request.

**What is original?** The presentation and classroom sequence are the project’s focus. Peer instruction and collecting student explanations are established ideas; we do not claim to have invented them.

**What is the next useful experiment?** Ask a lecturer to run one authored question in a small seminar. Compare human and AI categories, whether suggested prompts support productive discussion, and whether students can explain a new example. Avoid claiming educational effectiveness from the hackathon demo.
